    Compensation

    Uncompensated, partial or full

    When the lungs or kidneys throw the pH off, the other one pushes back to fix it. That push is compensation. Lungs change CO₂ in minutes; kidneys change HCO₃⁻ over days.

    1. Check the pHLow = acidosis, high = alkalosis. Normal? Still do step 3.
    2. Find the causeThe value that matches the pH is the problem (ROME).
    3. Check the other valueStill normal, or has it moved to help?

    One problem (respiratory acidosis) at three stages. Red = abnormal.

    Uncompensated

    Other value normal · pH abnormal

    7.25pH60PaCO₂23HCO₃⁻

    The kidneys haven’t responded yet. A sudden problem, like an opioid overdose.

    Partially compensated

    Other value moving · pH still abnormal

    7.32pH60PaCO₂30HCO₃⁻

    The kidneys are holding on to HCO₃⁻, but the pH isn’t back yet.

    Fully compensated

    Other value moved · pH back to normal

    7.37pH60PaCO₂34HCO₃⁻

    pH is fixed, but both values are still off. A long-standing problem, like COPD.

    Fully compensated? Look at 7.40

    7.35–7.39 leans acid → it started as acidosis. 7.41–7.45 leans alkaline → it started as alkalosis.

    Compensation or mixed?

    CO₂ and HCO₃⁻ both high or both low → compensation. One high, one low → mixed (two problems at once), not compensation.

    Oxyhemoglobin Dissociation Curve

    How PaO₂ relates to SaO₂

    The 60 / 90 rule
    A PaO₂ of 60 mmHg ≈ SaO₂ of 90%. Above 60 the curve is flat, so raising the PaO₂ adds little saturation. Below 60 it’s steep, so a small drop in PaO₂ causes a big drop in saturation. That’s why PaO₂ 60 / SpO₂ 90% is the usual minimum target.
    • Normal (P50 ≈ 27)
    • Left shift
    • Right shift
    PaO₂ 60 → SaO₂ ≈ 91%
    27 → 50%P50: PaO₂ where Hb is half saturated
    40 → 75%Normal mixed venous blood (PvO₂ / SvO₂)
    60 → 90%The 60 / 90 rule: minimum target
    100 → 97–98%Normal arterial blood
    Right shift · P50 > 27

    Hb lets go of O₂ more easily, so tissues get more. Lower SaO₂ at the same PaO₂.

    • ↑ PaCO₂
    • Acidosis (↓ pH, ↑ H⁺)
    • ↑ 2,3-DPG
    • Exercise
    • ↑ Temperature (fever)

    “CADET, face Right!” CO₂, Acid, 2,3-DPG, Exercise, Temperature.

    Left shift · P50 < 27

    Hb holds on to O₂ more tightly, so less is released to tissues. Higher SaO₂ at the same PaO₂.

    • ↓ PaCO₂
    • Alkalosis (↑ pH)
    • ↓ 2,3-DPG (e.g. banked blood)
    • ↓ Temperature (hypothermia)
    • Carbon monoxide (COHb), methemoglobin, fetal Hb

    Think of it as the opposite of CADET.

    Low flow · nasal cannula, simple mask
    Doesn’t meet the patient’s full inspiratory demand, so room air mixes in. Faster RR or bigger VT pulls in more room air → lower FiO₂; slower, shallower breathing → higher FiO₂.
    High flow · partial rebreather, non-rebreather, Venturi, HFNC
    Venturi and HFNC supply the patient’s entire inspiratory demand at a set O₂ concentration, so FiO₂ stays the same no matter how they breathe. The reservoir masks (partial and non-rebreather) still change FiO₂ with RR / VT. Venturi: best for COPD / CO₂ retainers needing a precise, low FiO₂. HFNC: high FiO₂ plus a small PEEP effect and dead-space washout. How to compute total flow →

    Large-volume nebulizer (aerosol) devices

    An air-entrainment nebulizer that gives O₂ plus bland aerosol (cool or heated mist). It connects to four interfaces: aerosol mask, face tent, trach collar and T-piece.

    Flowmeter10 – 15 L/min
    FiO₂ (set on the dial)28 – 100%
    Flow typeHigh, if total flow is enough
    HumidityAerosol: needed when the nose is bypassed

    Aerosol mask

    Use for: humidified O₂ over the nose and mouth: after extubation, thick secretions, upper-airway swelling (cool mist).

    The big holes let room air in, so at high settings the real FiO₂ can be lower than the dial.

    Face tent

    Use for: facial burns or trauma, or anyone who can’t tolerate a mask. It sits loosely under the chin.

    Open at the top, so FiO₂ is unpredictable. Don’t use it when you need an exact FiO₂.

    Trach collar trach mask

    Use for: a patient with a tracheostomy who breathes on their own. The trach bypasses the nose, so they need the humidity.

    Drain condensation away from the stoma, never toward it.

    T-piece Briggs adapter

    Use for: connects straight to an ET or trach tube: spontaneous breathing trials and weaning off the vent.

    Add reservoir tubing (50–150 mL) on the exhaust side so room air isn’t pulled in.

    Total flow (trach collar and the other three)
    Total flow = O₂ flow × (air + O₂ parts), and it must be at least 3 × the patient’s minute ventilation (usually ≥ 40 L/min for an adult). Example: trach collar at 40%, flowmeter 12 L/min → 3 : 1 → 4 parts × 12 = 48 L/min ✓. Quick check: mist should keep coming out of the exhaust or mask holes when the patient breathes in. If it disappears, flow is too low. Total flow formula →
